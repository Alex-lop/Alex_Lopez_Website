Graphene

A visual way to see what you can build next with an agent

Graphene on GitHub

I believe that in the near future, whether through new models, better harnesses, or a shift off the LLM frame entirely (Jev, recently), the limit on building software won't be tokens or cost. The limiting factor will be the human intervention and drive it takes to actually get the software out. I know this sounds a little wild. The conversation is still mostly "who has the better model?" and "what's the best harness?" For most applications, though, I think the current models are already completely capable of debugging and fixing the work.

Ok, but not to ramble on too long, that idea is what drove me to build Graphene. No matter how many tokens you have, if you don't say which direction the application should go, the agent has to infer most of it. Graphene tries to bridge that connection between you and the agent. So, not claiming it magically saves 397% of your tokens but (ideally) it should help guide you and the agent toward that perfect idolized application you actually had in mind from the start.

Astra came in about 10× leaner than Fable 5.1 and 42× leaner than Opus 5. Jev lands in a similar accuracy band at a cost that barely shows up. Tokens and dollars are already getting cheap. The part that still takes a person is knowing where the work should go.

What's the future of math with AI?

I think the future of math is genuinely uncertain. Four years ago, in my Physics C class, ChatGPT could not solve a single one of my homework problems. Now it takes me hours just to wrap my head around one paragraph, or one theorem, that it wrote in Lean.

The beauty of math, and of science more generally, is the process of solving a problem and actually sitting in it. A lot of what I see from the math community online comes down to one line, and I'll paraphrase it: AI kills the allure of math. If you can just get to an answer and have it formalized, what's the point of doing it yourself when all you care about is the solution?

Terence Tao said something close to this while he was teaching. I don't remember the exact talk, so this is a paraphrase: no one really understands the Navier-Stokes solution, and there is no urgency to rush these problems just to have them solved. AI is bad in that regard. The benefits are still too large to doubt. If you told a mathematician they could have the most knowledgeable assistant they had ever had, someone who could recite any fact, they would take it. AI is not that. It doesn't allow for the same natural comprehension.

Not to ramble too long. If you are still willing to uncover these problems and challenge them, I do think AI can help, and that you should use it.
